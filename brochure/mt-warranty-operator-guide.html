🛡️ Warranty Program — Operator Guide

How the parts & labor warranty works, what lives where in DSM, and exactly what to do at each moment. Written July 2026.

The big picture in one paragraph

Every install gets the manufacturer's 10-year parts warranty (we register it) and our 2-year labor warranty (free, automatic, our own promise). Customers on our $29/mo maintenance plan get labor stretched to 5 years — the warranty is the hook that sells the plan, and the plan is the recurring revenue that pays for the warranty. Customers who want certainty can buy a 10-year labor policy (third-party insured, one-time price on the quote). That's the whole program.

Where each piece lives

📄 The Warranty Sheet

Printable HTML doc (mt-installation-warranty.html). Customer-facing. Print or PDF it into every install quote packet. Has a signature block with a Warranty Tier line.

🔧 The Job Record

DSM → Jobs → job detail. Install jobs show Warranty: and Registered: lines. Fields: tier, provider, policy #, registration date. Red ⚠ warning if a completed install isn't registered.

📋 The Contracts Page

DSM → Contracts. The customer's $29/mo plan lives here as a Residential Protection Plan row. Its active status IS the 5-year warranty — cancelled plan = coverage reverts to 2 years.

Workflow ① — Quoting an install

1
Include the warranty sheet in the quote packet.
Print/PDF mt-installation-warranty.html
2
Say the one-sentence pitch.
Your install comes with the manufacturer's 10-year parts warranty — I register that for you — plus 2 years labor from me, free. Most of my customers go on the maintenance plan, $29 a month, two tune-ups a year, and that stretches your labor coverage to 5 years. If you want labor locked for the full 10 no matter what, that's a one-time option I'll price on your quote.
3
Two independent yes/no questions. Plan? (→ 5yr) Full 10-year lock? (→ policy). They stack: plan + 10-year is the best customer. And a 10-year buyer should STILL take the plan — documented maintenance is what keeps every warranty claim valid.

Workflow ② — Closing an install (the checklist)

1
Customer signs the warranty sheet with their tier circled/written on the Warranty Tier line. Photo of it onto the job (job attachments).
2
Register the equipment with the manufacturer — within 7 days. Unregistered equipment usually gets only 5-yr parts instead of 10. Then record the date on the job.
Job detail shows a red ⚠ NOT REGISTERED on completed installs until this is done.
3
If they took the plan: create their contract. Contracts page → the plan row should exist via their Stripe signup, or add it manually. Plan must start within 30 days of install for the 5-year tier to apply.
4
If they bought the 10-year: purchase the JB policy on that equipment (deadline: usually 60-90 days after install — do it same week). Record provider + policy # on the job.
5
Set the warranty fields on the job. Tier, provider, policy #, registration date.
There's no form for these yet — for now it's a one-line SQL update, or ask Claude. Building the dropdown + "mark registered" button + auto-checklist is the next dead-zone project.

Workflow ③ — A warranty call comes in (year 1-10)

1
Search the job (Jobs page → search customer name/phone). Read the Warranty line: tier, policy #, registered date.
2
Check coverage by tier: Within 2 years of install → covered, period. Tier says 5yr-plan → glance at Contracts: plan active = covered · cancelled/past-due = coverage reverted to the 2-year window. Tier says 10yr → covered; do the repair, then file the labor claim with JB using the policy # (check their pre-authorization rules before major repairs like compressors).
3
Covered call = no labor charge, no trip charge. This is the moment the program earns its reputation — a warranty call handled instantly and graciously creates a customer for life and a review.
Watch for the lapse alert: if a plan member's card fails, Telegram pings the DSM group ("⚠ PLAN PAYMENT FAILED"). Stripe retries automatically — but if it fails again, call the customer. A lapsed plan quietly kills their 5-year coverage, and finding that out during a breakdown is the worst possible moment.
